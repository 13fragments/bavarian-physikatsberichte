## Single post

Swabia, around 1860. A Danube doctor on a marshy village: "Since the Danube runs deeper and its river area was regulated, and since the pond dried out, the force of the malaria that prevails there is broken." He blamed bad air. The mosquito link came in the 1890s.

## Thread

1. Malaria was endemic in 19th-century Bavaria. Around 1860 the district doctors called it *Wechselfieber*, intermittent fever, and described it along the Danube, in the marshes, and in a Rhine fortress.

2. Lauingen, on the Danube: "In earlier years, in spring and autumn, intermittent fevers were very frequent in the places on the Danube, and they mostly appeared as tertian fever."

3. Then it changed: "For several years, however, these intermittent fevers have appeared to a far smaller extent." He credits the deeper Danube, "the river regulation and the rarer floods that result from it", and the "drying of backwaters, swamps, and ditches."

4. About one village, Peterswörth, he is blunter: "Since the Danube runs deeper and its river area was regulated, and since the pond dried out, the force of the malaria that prevails there is broken."

5. His theory was wrong. Monheim, downriver, states it plainly: after floods, decaying plant and animal matter produces "the so-called malaria", Italian for "bad air", "and the diseases that arise from it, especially intermittent fever."

6. The real cause, a parasite carried by *Anopheles* mosquitoes, was not discovered until the 1880s and 1890s. But the doctors' measure was right: no standing water, no mosquito larvae.

7. Kastl, Upper Palatinate: "Most of the intermittent fevers have the simple or double tertian type; the quotidian type is rare and the quartan type rarer still." A fever every third day fits *Plasmodium vivax*, the milder malaria of temperate Europe.

8. The scale: the military hospital of the Rhine fortress of Germersheim treated 10,197 cases of intermittent fever from October 1848 to March 1858. In the town's civil hospital, about one patient in five had it.

9. Inland, the fever was a souvenir. Karlstadt, on the Main: "Intermittent fevers are a true rarity and affect almost only soldiers who have returned from infected garrison towns."

10. And folk medicine? Riedenburg's list of village remedies, number 49: "Willow bark, good for intermittent fever." Willow bark contains salicin, a cousin of aspirin. It lowers the fever, but it does not kill the parasite. Quinine from cinchona bark does.

11. Source: the 1858-1861 Bavarian district physicians' reports (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Cgm 6874), open data: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24523214

## Original German

- Lauingen, district (`bsb00009733` canvases 67-70, viewer images 67-70): "In frühern Jahren waren im Frühjahre und Herbste in den an der Donau gelegenen Orten die Wechselfieber sehr häufig, welche meist als Tertian-Fieber auftraten. [...] Seit mehreren Jahren erscheinen aber diese Wechselfieber in weit geringerer Extensität, was vorzüglich dem auch seit einigen Jahren beobachteten tiefern Gange der Donau, der Flußregulirung, und den dadurch bedingten seltenern [?] Ueberschwemmungen, sowie dem natürlichen u. künstlichen Austrocknen von Altwässern, Sümpfen und Gräben zuzuschreiben ist."
- Lauingen, Peterswörth (`bsb00009733` canvas 174): "Früher war es häufigen Ueberschwemmungen durch die Donau ausgesetzt, und wurde dadurch, sowie durch die Exhalationen eines im Orte selbst befindlichen Weihers eine ergiebige Quelle für Wechselfieber. Seit dem tiefern Gange der Donau und Regulirung des Stromgebietes, sowie seit Austrocknung des Weihers ist die Kraft der dort herrschenden Malaria gebrochen."
- Monheim (`bsb00094713` canvases 66-67): "Durch diese Überschwemmungen vorzüglich der Donau werden sowohl vegetabilische als animalische Stoffe ausgeworfen, dem Fäulnißprocesse unterworfen, dadurch die sogenannte Malaria und die daraus entstehenden Krankheiten, besonders Wechselfieber erzeugt."
- Kastl (`bsb00020843` canvas 86): "Die Wechselfieber haben in der Mehrzahl den einfachen oder doppelten Tertiantypus, selten ist der Quotidian und noch seltener der Quartan-Typus."
- Germersheim (`bsb00140616` canvases 83-84): "Summa von October 1848 bis Ende März 1858 in Behandlung gewesener Wechselfieberkranker 10,197"; civil hospital, yearly average: "auf 100 Kranke kommen jährlich 21,16 Wechselfieberkranke".
- Karlstadt (`bsb00144876` canvas 459): "Wechselfieber sind eine wahre Seltenheit und betreffen beinahe nur aus inficirten Garnisonsplätzen zurückgekehrte Soldaten."
- Riedenburg (`bsb00146476` canvas 104): "49. Weidenrinde gut für Wechselfieber."

## Caveats

- **Viewer links for Lauingen.** In volume `bsb00009733` the library's manifest lists most images twice, so the viewer counts 282 pages for about 140 images. The dataset canvas number equals the viewer page number; `?page=174` shows image 87, the right page. Do not "correct" the link to `?page=87`, which shows a different page.
- The decline is the Lauingen physician's judgment, without counts or dates. Monheim reports that the Danube swamps shrank after the river correction, but not that the fever declined there; it says the fever "has become endemic".
- "Peterswörth" is a village in the Lauingen district. The pond was there, not in Lauingen itself.
- Germersheim: 10,197 is the military hospital total for 9½ years; the table gives no garrison size, so no rate per soldier. "One in five" is the civil-hospital yearly average (21.16 per 100 patients, 1858-1860). Germersheim is on the Rhine, not the Danube.
- Günzburg (`bsb00144874` canvas 240) lists 15 deaths from "pernicious" intermittent fever in six years between 1851/52 and 1857/58. It is left out of the thread because the numbers are small and the diagnosis is his.
- Thread parts 6, 7 (the *vivax* reading), and 10 (salicin, quinine) are modern context, not in the reports. The discovery dates (Laveran 1880 for the parasite; Ross 1897 and Grassi 1898 for mosquito transmission) are external; check them before you post.
