# Post 2: A doctor around 1860 tests the Year Without a Summer, and finds no clear effect

- **Type:** main post (thread).
- **Status:** all quotes and numbers checked against the page images (verification log: `verification/conscripts.md`). One earlier claim failed: the Erlangen physician did not find more bodily defects in the famine cohorts. He found no effect at all. This draft reports that null result.
- **Image:** Erlangen Stadt, `bsb00140610` canvas 68, crop of the three sentences from "Die Zahl der Conscribirten ist selbst von den 1816 und 1817 Gebornen" to "Mindermäßige sind gar keine dabei." Viewer: https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/de/view/bsb00140610?page=68

## Single post

1816, the "Year Without a Summer", brought hunger to southern Germany. Around 1860 the Erlangen doctor checked the army records of men born in 1816-17: not noticeably fewer, no noticeably more defects, and "no undersized men among them at all." His explanation: industrial wages.

## Thread

1. In 1815 the Tambora volcano erupted. 1816 became the "Year Without a Summer", and 1816-17 brought dearth and famine to southern Germany. Statisticians of the time had a rule: children born in hunger years grow into fewer, weaker, shorter recruits.

2. Around 1860 the district doctor of Erlangen, in Franconia, decided to check that rule against his town's conscription records, which list the young men called up each year, their height, and the reasons they were rejected.

3. His finding: "The number of conscripts, even of those born in 1816 and 1817, is not noticeably smaller, and just as little is the number of bodily defects noticeably larger. There are no undersized men among them at all."

4. A null result, around 1860. He did not stop there. He agreed with a Würzburg colleague that wages from industry protect a town against dearth better than farm work does.

5. The same records give the height of Erlangen's men: on average 5 feet 7 inches 6 lines over 20 birth years. If he used the Bavarian foot, that is about 164 cm. The tallest year-class was born in 1823, the shortest in 1832 to 1834.

6. Other districts reported their own numbers. Amberg: of 581 men measured over ten years, 73 were over 6 feet (about 175 cm), the tallest 6′7″6‴ (about 193 cm), and only 16 fell short of the 5′4″ minimum (about 156 cm).

7. Height was rarely the problem. Uffenheim: per 10,000 conscripts in the kingdom, 195 were rejected as too short but 2,182 for bodily defects. In rural Uffenheim the rates were 149 and 1,287, which the doctor put down to a purely rural population.

8. And the defects? Eschenbach lists the main ones: consumption of the lungs, hernias, goiters, and, "especially in the towns", short sight.

9. Source: the 1858-1861 Bavarian district physicians' reports (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Cgm 6874), open data: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24523214

## Caveats

- The Erlangen statement is a judgment ("not noticeably"), based on his Appendix 8, which we have not checked. He prints no numbers for the 1816 and 1817 cohorts in the text. His mean-height table starts with men born in 1819, so "no undersized men" is his only height statement about the famine cohorts.
- He names no industry. His words are only "earnings from industry" (*Verdienst durch Industrie*). Do not add examples without a source.
- The physicians do not name the foot. The centimetre values assume the Bavarian foot (29.19 cm, 12 inches of 12 lines). Keep the word "about" or drop the conversions.
- Do not quote the Erlangen first-period mean "5′10″7‴". It is in the ink but contradicts his own figures; it is very probably a slip of the pen.
- Amberg: "581 in the ten-year average" is a ten-year total; the "Normal Maaß" of 5′4″ is the minimum height for service.
- Eschenbach writes that "fairly exactly the 4th part" was unfit, but his own counts are 31 of 166 (19%). That is why this draft quotes his list of defects but not his share.
- Tambora and the 1816-17 dearth are external context, not in the reports. This draft does not state the conscription age, because the reports checked here do not give it.
- A separate Erlangen finding (canvas 67) could make a follow-up: the share of men too short rose sharply everywhere in the conscription years 1852-57 (Erlangen 6.5%, Middle Franconia 5.65%, Bavaria 4.16%). The physician does not explain it. Do not supply an explanation without a source.
