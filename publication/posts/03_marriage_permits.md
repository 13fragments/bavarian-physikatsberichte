# Post 3: Love vetoed by the village council

- **Type:** main post (thread).
- **Status:** all six quotes checked against the page images (verification log: `verification/marriage.md`). Every passage is one physician's opinion or estimate, not a count.
- **Image:** Marktheidenfeld, `bsb00145124` canvas 179, crop of the last sentence ("Wird einst den Armen das Heurathen erlaubt werden ..."). Viewer: https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/de/view/bsb00145124?page=179

## Single post

In 1861 a Franconian doctor explained why some local women lived in America: the village would not let poor couples marry, so they sailed across the Atlantic to keep their promise. "When one day the poor are allowed to marry, there will be no more Franconian American women."

## Thread

1. In 1860s Bavaria, a poor couple could not simply marry. They needed the municipality's permission to settle, and the council could refuse it. In 1858 the state asked every district physician to describe his district. Several of them wrote about what this rule did to people.

2. Marktheidenfeld, Lower Franconia: "only a few, who could not marry here and yet wanted to stay true to their promises, went to America to enter into marriage there. [...] When one day the poor are allowed to marry, there will be no more Franconian American women."

3. Viechtach, Lower Bavaria, on how the veto worked: "Many honest people who could make a good living are not allowed to marry, because one or another of the municipal representatives is their enemy; rogues, however, are feared, and these get their settlement permit sooner."

4. The same physician blamed the veto for the "many illegitimate children" in his district. Couples did not stop having children. They stopped being allowed to marry.

5. Neustadt an der Waldnaab, on the cost to the village: after a refused marriage, the support of mother and children "mostly remains with the municipalities" if the family became destitute. With a marriage, the husband would have carried that burden.

6. Windsheim, Middle Franconia: "far more marriages would be entered into if settlement were not made so very difficult." Most men there married at 30 to 40, most women at 25 to 30.

7. And those who did get the permit married regardless of money. Nittenau, Upper Palatinate: everyone marries "who receives the permission to marry, even if he is ever so poor." Many women there gave birth 14 to 16 times; 6, 8 or 10 births were the rule.

8. Source: the 1858-1861 Bavarian district physicians' reports (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Cgm 6874), transcribed in full and openly released: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24523214

## Caveats

- Every passage is one physician's view. None gives a count of refused permits or of emigrants.
- Marktheidenfeld signs his report on 29 November 1861 (canvas 181), so the single post says 1861.
- Marktheidenfeld describes emigration as rare in his district. The "Franconian American women" are a few cases, not a mass movement. One word in his middle sentence is uncertain; the quoted sentences are clear.
- Hersbruck (`bsb00109173` canvas 136) links emigration to the lack of a livelihood, which was the legal condition for a marriage permit. He also says that not all emigrants found their fortune in America, and that this news ended the wish to follow them. Use it only with that nuance.
- Viechtach hedges ("as it seems"). Quote him as blaming the veto, not as proving it.
- "Fallen persons" (Neustadt) is the physician's moral term for unmarried mothers. Keep the quotation marks if you use it.

## External context (check before posting)

- The marriage restrictions were relaxed by the Bavarian law on home rights, marriage and residence of 1868. Historical demographers link them to Bavaria's high illegitimacy rate; see John Knodel, "Law, marriage and illegitimacy in nineteenth-century Germany," *Population Studies* 20(3), 1967.
