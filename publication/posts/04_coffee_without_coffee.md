## Single post

Kronach, around 1860. Raftsmen eat sausage at the inn; their families at home "make do with potatoes and chicory coffee." One merchant sells 300 hundredweight of chicory a year (about 17 tonnes), "and besides him there are 8 more merchants here whose businesses do equally well."

## Thread

1. Around 1860, Bavaria's district doctors had to describe what people ate and drank. Again and again they describe "coffee" that contains almost no coffee.

2. Schillingsfürst gives the recipe: "to a few real beans one takes a handful of diced and dried mangel-beet root [?] together with some chicory, and after boiling mixes it with much milk and a little sugar." A brew "which does not, or hardly, deserve the name."

3. Kronach, Upper Franconia: chicory coffee is "a very common one among the poorer class, and it is astonishing what quantities of chicory the local merchants sell." One merchant: 300 hundredweight a year, about 17 tonnes. Eight more "do equally well."

4. Dahn, in the Palatinate, puts it in numbers: "One can assume that more than half of the meals in the canton consist of such coffee-coloured water with a little milk – and potatoes or bread."

5. Nürnberg, on class: "While the rich man sips one, at most two cups of admittedly strong coffee in the morning, the poorer drink a Seidlein, often even more, of their brown liquid with the same enjoyment." A Seidlein was about half a litre.

6. Not every doctor approved. Erbendorf: substitutes "such as chicory, mangel beets, carrots, and roasted grain [...] not seldom have harmful consequences for health." The Nürnberg doctor disagreed: no city breakfast was cheaper, tastier, easier to make or healthier.

7. And at the village church fair near Erlangen, the lads treated their girls to beer and, "when they have had enough of that, to coffee drunk from one-Maß beer mugs." Each mug held about a litre.

8. Source: the 1858-1861 Bavarian district physicians' reports (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Cgm 6874), open data: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24523214

## Original German

Denn zu einigen ächten Bohnen wird eine Hand voll gewürfelter und getrockneter Rangers-Wurzel [?] nebst etwas Cichorie genommen, und nach dem Abkochen mit viel Milch und etwas Zucker vermischt [...]"
- Dahn (`bsb00140604` canvas 51): "Am allgemeinsten verbreitet ist das „Kaffeetrinken“, das jedoch nicht nach der Caffea arabica, sondern nach Kaffeesurrogaten so genannt wird. - Nürnberg (`bsb00145138` canvas 59): "Während der Reiche eine, höchstens zwei Tassen allerdings starken Kaffees morgens schlürft, trinken die Aermeren ein Seidlein oft noch mehr ihrer braunen Flüssigkeit mit demselben Behagen."

## Caveats

- Kronach: the 300 hundredweight is his report of local knowledge, without a source. A Bavarian Centner was 56 kg, so 300 Centner is about 17 tonnes; the conversion is ours. He says the 8 other merchants "do equally well", not that each sells 300 Centner. Do not multiply.
- Dahn: "more than half of the meals" is his estimate, and the meal is the drink together with potatoes or bread.
- Schillingsfürst: one word ("Rangers", a Franconian word for mangel beet) is uncertain in the ink.
- Nürnberg: the "healthier breakfast" defence (thread part 6) is on canvas 58, which also says that substitutes were added "for economy" in poorer households. Seidlein is about half a Maß; the litre value is ours.
- Erlangen Land: a church-fair custom. It does not say that this coffee was a substitute. The litre value is ours (1 Maß ≈ 1.07 litres).
- Stadtprozelten (`bsb00145146` canvas 415) has a vivid eyewitness scene: a pot of "coffee" (goat's milk with a brew of "German coffee") and steaming potatoes, and no salt because the pennies ran out. It is left out of the thread to keep it short. If you use it, do not call the brew chicory: he only says "deutscher Kaffee".
