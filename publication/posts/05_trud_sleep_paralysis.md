## Single post

Upper Palatinate, around 1860. Villagers tell the district doctor about the *Trud*: you hear her creep up "with a noise like blotting paper", she falls on you "with a hundredweight load", and "it is then impossible to speak a word." Today we call it sleep paralysis.

## Thread

1. Around 1860 the district doctor of Parsberg, in the Upper Palatinate, wrote down what people told him about the *Druden*. They "fall upon people in bed as if with a leaden weight and press them."

2. The sufferers "claim that they hear them creep up with a noise like blotting paper or tinsel, and fall upon them with a hundredweight load." And: "it is then impossible to speak a word. When another person speaks to someone so tormented, he is instantly freed of his burden."

3. That is a precise description of sleep paralysis: you wake while the body's dream paralysis lasts a little longer. People feel pressure on the chest, sense a presence, hear noises, and cannot speak. Large surveys find that a sizable minority have it at least once.

4. The doctor of Eschenbach gives the medical name in brackets: "The so-called Trud-pressing (nightmare pressure) also comes from witches, according to the prevailing opinion." German *Alpdrücken*, the old word for nightmare, means "elf-pressing".

5. Who was the Trud? Dingolfing: "Truden and witches, the former inborn, the latter acquired, are believed unshakeably." Burgau: they ride people and cattle in their sleep; the horse is found in the morning "faint and exhausted, with foam at its mouth, tail and mane tousled."

6. Protection: a "Trud cross" cut into the bedstead above the head (Wegscheid). A small star sign, the *Drudenfuß*, on doors, windows and bedsteads (Kötzting). For a woman in childbed, a piece of her husband's clothing (Parsberg).

7. Or catch her. Lauingen: if a witch chokes you at night, throw your pillow on the floor; she stays sitting on it. "If you seize the Drude or witch with your hands and do not let her go, she turns into a straw."

8. Here the belief turns dangerous. Parsberg: shout after her "In three devils' names, come tomorrow!", and the Trud must appear in person the next day. "In this way people claim to have discovered many Truden already, who were always old women."

9. Wegscheid: "if a child's breasts swell, the Trud has suckled on them." Remedy: a mirror and nine kinds of dung in the swaddling cloth. Newborns often get harmless breast swelling from their mother's hormones, sometimes with a little milk. Folk name: "witch's milk".

10. Source: the 1858-1861 Bavarian district physicians' reports (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Cgm 6874), open data: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24523214

## Original German

- Parsberg (`bsb00003359` canvas 130-131): "Diese Druden üben ihre Gewalt auch über andere Menschen, indem sie die Menschen wie mit einer bleiernen Last im Bette überfallen und drücken. Solche von ihnen geplagte Menschen behaupten, daß sie dieselben mit einem Geräusch, wie Löschpapier, Rauschgold heranschleichen und mit zentnerschwerer Last auf sie hinfallen hören. [...] Man versichert, daß es dann unmöglich sey, ein Wort zu sprechen. Wenn ein anderer einen solchen Gequälten anspricht, wird er Augenblicklich von seiner Last befreit." And: "Schreit man dann gleich nach: „In drei Teufelsnamen kom morgen“, so muß sich die Trude den andern Tag in Person stellen. Auf diese Weise will man schon viele Truden entdeckt haben, die immer alte Weiber waren."

- Burgau (`bsb00140602` canvas 168): "Die Macht der Truden ist denselben angeboren, und besteht darin, Menschen und Vieh im Schlaf zu reiten, zu drücken, zu ängstigen [...]; von den Thieren reiten sie zumal das Roß, das man dann am Morgen ganz matt und erschöpft, mit Schaum vor dem Munde, Schweif und Mähnen verzaust [?] und verwirrt, im Stalle findet."

## Caveats

- Every passage reports popular belief. The physicians keep their distance ("people claim", "according to the prevailing opinion").
- Thread parts 3, 4 (the meaning of *Alpdrücken*), and 9 (newborn breast swelling, "witch's milk") are modern interpretation, not in the reports. If you want a number for how common sleep paralysis is, add a source before you post it.
- Wegscheid says "Kinde" (child). The swaddling cloth points to an infant, but the physician gives no medical reading.
- Kötzting draws the sign in the line, but it is too small to count five points. Call it a small star sign. Do not repeat his claim that *Druden* comes from the Celtic Druids.
- Parsberg canvas 134 is already quoted in the folk medicine article draft (`publication/article_2_folk_medicine_outline_and_quotes.md`, the night watchman's wife). This post uses canvases 129-131 only.
